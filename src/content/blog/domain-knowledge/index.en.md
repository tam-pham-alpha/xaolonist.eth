Hey hey, it's him again.

People used to think a senior engineer was someone who could solve the hardest technical problems. Whoever could build the largest, most optimized, cleanest systems would be the most valuable. He used to think that too. But the more he worked in specialized domains, the more he realized that programming skill may only be a necessary condition. What is truly scarce is the ability to understand an industry deeply enough.

As AI gets better at writing code, the value of only knowing how to program starts to decline. Repeated code patterns, new APIs, and new frameworks can all be learned very quickly. AI can even learn them faster than humans. What AI still cannot compress is decades of accumulated experience inside one domain. Because domain knowledge does not live in documentation. It lives in thousands of small decisions made every day.

## Engineering experience and industry experience are two different assets

Imagine two engineers, each with ten years of experience. One mostly builds e-commerce products. The other builds trading infrastructure for investment funds. If they swapped seats, both would likely struggle. Not because they cannot program, but because they are missing another language. The language of the industry.

In finance, even understanding why an order was rejected can take years. Why this venue requires IOC, while that one uses FOK. Why a price delayed by a few hundred milliseconds can create millions of dollars of risk. Why a system always prioritizes correctness over speed in some places, yet accepts trade-offs elsewhere. Those answers are not in programming textbooks. They are formed from market history, regulation, participant behavior, and incidents that have already happened.

## Why companies still hunt for people who have stayed in one domain for a long time

That is also why many large financial firms open offices in Malaysia or India. Not exactly because they cannot find senior engineers in Vietnam. Vietnam has many strong engineers who work hard and learn very quickly. What is harder to find are people who have both ten years of experience building systems and ten years living inside electronic trading, investment banking, or market infrastructure.

Engineering experience and domain knowledge are actually two completely different accumulation paths. Programming experience often grows with years in the craft. But domain knowledge only grows when you stay long enough in the same field, see enough problems, and understand why that industry operates the way it does today. Someone can become a senior engineer in seven years. But to become someone who truly understands financial markets, insurance, or aviation, it may take another seven years.

## AI can learn code very quickly, but it cannot live ten years in your place

That is why in many companies, there are people who no longer write as much code as before, yet every important decision still needs their input. They are not necessarily the fastest coders. They are simply the ones who can see consequences that others do not yet see. A tiny change in a system can trigger a chain of effects downstream, and those links only become visible after someone has lived long enough inside that domain.

AI can read millions of documents, but it cannot truly live through a liquidity crisis, a production incident that lasts for hours, or watch an entire industry shift because of a new regulation. Those experiences do not only create knowledge. They form intuition. And intuition usually appears after many rounds of being right, being wrong, and paying the price.

## An engineer's moat is changing place

Seen through a business lens, every engineer also has a kind of **moat**, a personal economic moat. In the past, that moat was often the ability to write better code than others: knowing more languages, optimizing better, shipping faster. In the AI era, that layer of moat is getting thinner very quickly, because code is becoming something that can be produced at an ever lower cost.

The new moat, if it still exists, is almost no longer a single skill. It is the product of a few things that are hard to buy with a course or a prompt:

`domain knowledge × network × reputation × decision-making`

Domain knowledge helps you see the right problem. Network helps you reach the right information and opportunities at the right time. Reputation helps others trust your judgment when the data is still fuzzy. Decision-making turns understanding into action before the window closes. Missing one of these factors, the edge can collapse quickly. Domain without reputation makes it hard to be given authority. Network without decision-making leaves only conversation.

This also matches what he is observing about the new order. When AI makes information and code spread almost instantly, advantage no longer sits in how fast you can produce an artifact. It sits in who still has enough context to know which artifact is worth producing, and when not to produce one at all.

## An engineer's competitive advantage is changing

Maybe this is also the largest shift in the programming profession in the AI era. In the past, competitive advantage sat in knowing how to build a system. In the future, advantage will sit in understanding why that system must be built that way.

One day, the most important interview question will no longer be: "Which programming languages do you know?"

It will be: "How many years have you spent understanding the industry we operate in?"

Because code will be written by AI faster every day. But the years it takes to understand a domain have no shortcut. That may be the most valuable asset an engineer can accumulate across an entire career.
