Hey hey, it's him again.

He used to think the best people were the ones who always made the right decisions. Later, after investing, writing software, and watching how large companies get built, he realized that may not be the most important thing. What matters more is knowing how to find **asymmetric opportunities**.

Those are situations where if you are wrong, you lose very little. But if you are right, the reward can be many times larger than what you put in.

It sounds simple, but the more he thinks about it, the more he sees that almost every major turning point in life takes that shape.

## The world does not reward those who are right the most

There is something fairly interesting here. In school, we are taught to chase the highest scores and make as few mistakes as possible. But outside school, rewards are not divided that way.

Someone who invests correctly 6 out of 10 times will not necessarily make more money than someone who is right only 3 times. A company can fail with dozens of products, yet one successful product can be enough to change the whole company. A venture fund can lose money on most startups, yet a single investment like Nvidia, Alibaba, or Facebook can be enough to cover the entire portfolio.

The world does not seem to reward those who are right the most. It rewards those who know how to bet on opportunities with the most attractive payoff ratios.

That is the essence of an asymmetric opportunity.

## Asymmetric opportunities are everywhere

The interesting part is that asymmetric opportunities do not only exist in financial markets.

A coffee with a stranger can also be a bet like that. At worst, he loses an afternoon and a few cups of coffee. But maybe the person across the table becomes a cofounder, a first customer, a guide, or even a life partner. The cost to try is very small, yet the value can last for decades.

Writing a blog post is the same. Hundreds of posts may get only a few dozen readers. But one post that spreads at the right moment can bring thousands of readers, collaboration invites, career opportunities, or friends he had never met. The cost is only a few hours of writing, yet the value can keep compounding for years.

Even moving to a big city is sometimes an asymmetric decision. Rent may be higher, life may be more stressful. But the number of people, ideas, and opportunities he can stumble into grows exponentially. Sometimes luck is not something you wait for. It is the result of placing yourself where opportunities are more likely to appear.

## Small bets that change a life

Looking more closely, he sees that successful people often do not try to do only sure things. They simply keep placing small bets.

Learn one more skill. Write one more post. Attend one more conference. Meet one more person. Invest an amount of money that, if lost, would not meaningfully hurt their life.

Most of those bets will not bring anything special. But they understand that if only a few of them succeed, every cost before that becomes worthwhile.

By contrast, many of us spend most of our time on symmetric bets, or even on bets that are asymmetric in the wrong direction. Staying in a job with little room to grow just because it feels safe. Avoiding something new because we fear losing a few months. Not meeting strangers because we fear rejection. Not sharing our thoughts because we fear a few judgments.

The risk is very small, yet we give up rewards that could be very large.

## What matters is not always being right

Maybe that is because the human brain was built more to avoid loss than to seek opportunity. We feel more pain from losing 100 thousand dong than joy from gaining 100 thousand dong. That instinct once helped our ancestors survive, but in a world that changes very quickly, it also makes us more likely to miss the most valuable opportunities.

He also realized something else. An asymmetric opportunity is not the same as being reckless. It is not putting your entire net worth into one coin, quitting a job with no plan, or betting everything on a single idea.

On the contrary, the most beautiful asymmetric opportunities usually share one trait: the cost to try is very low. He only spends a small portion of money, time, or effort in exchange for the chance of a much larger result.

Maybe that is also why people who build wealth over the long term are not too obsessed with how often they are right. They care more about how much they get back each time they are right.

Instead of trying to avoid every mistake, perhaps the better thing to do is keep looking for bets where if he is wrong, he can still smile and walk on. And if he is right, maybe just once is enough to open an entirely new trajectory for the rest of his life.
