Hey, it's him again.

Lately he has been spending quite a bit of time playing with Discord. At first he thought it was just an app for chat, gaming, and joining random communities. But the more he looked, the more he saw something interesting. Discord has already built most of the foundation that many future apps will need.

## When every app gradually becomes a conversation

In the past, each piece of software usually had its own interface. To book a flight, we opened a screen full of input fields. To find a job, we went through a long list of filters. To learn English, we picked lessons, clicked through menus, and moved screen by screen. Most of the work of building software also went into figuring out where users should click to tell the computer what they want.

AI is changing that fairly quickly.

Instead of learning how to use a piece of software, people can simply say what they want. "Find me a flight to Singapore this weekend." "Explain why this code is slow." "I want to learn about trading systems over the next three months." Computers are starting to learn how to understand humans, instead of humans having to learn how to use computers.

When that happens, chat has a real chance of becoming one of the most natural interfaces for software. Not because every app needs to look like ChatGPT, but because conversation is how humans have been communicating intent with each other for thousands of years.

Behind one message, hundreds of systems may be running. But in front of the user, there is only a small box with a blinking cursor.

## And Discord already has that box

This is what caught his attention about Discord.

If he wanted to build an app from scratch, he would have to handle signup, login, profiles, notifications, permissions, chat, realtime connections, community management, and a pile of other small things. Those may not create the core value of the product, but without them the product simply does not run.

Discord has already done most of that work.

It has users, servers, channels, roles, permissions, messages, voice, notifications, and a fairly mature bot system. More importantly, it is not an empty platform waiting for developers to bring users in. A very large number of users are already there, already used to joining servers, chatting with bots, receiving notifications, and moving between different communities.

So instead of building a new house in an empty field and trying to pull people over to live in it, a developer can open a shop in a city that is already crowded.

## API turns Discord into a platform

The next interesting part is that Discord does not only let bots reply to a few fun commands.

Its API lets developers interact with many core parts of the platform. Bots can read and send messages, create channels, manage roles, handle interactions, send notifications, control access, and connect to systems that live entirely outside Discord.

That creates a fairly clean boundary.

Discord handles what humans see and interact with every day. Developers handle the logic behind it.

A dating service, for example, could use Discord as the place where users join a community and chat. But matching, verification, payment, reputation, and AI could live on a separate server. When two people are a good fit, the bot simply notifies them. When both agree to connect, the system can open a private space for them.

From the user's side, everything happens inside Discord. But behind it is already a fairly complex application.

## The stronger AI gets, the thinner the interface layer

This is probably the part that excites him most.

AI is driving down the cost of building software logic very quickly. One developer can now build systems that used to require an entire team. At the same time, AI is also making many traditional interfaces unnecessary.

If users can say directly what they want, why build ten screens for them to click through?

If AI can understand what kind of person someone is looking for, why force them to drag twenty filter sliders?

If an AI tutor knows where a learner is, why should the learner open a curriculum and hunt for the next lesson?

Software may gradually become invisible. Users talk to the system, the system finds the right tools behind the scenes, and brings the result back into the conversation.

Then what matters is no longer who has the prettiest dashboard. It is who has better context, better workflow, better data, and a better community.

## Discord as an operating system for communities

Perhaps that is why he started looking at Discord differently.

Discord is not necessarily just a chat app. It can be viewed as a social infrastructure layer that other apps run on top of.

Dating is only one example. Another server could become a school with an AI tutor for each member. Another could become a professional club where engineers, traders, and founders are verified and connected. Another could become a prediction network where every call is stored for years to build each person's reputation.

Developers do not need to rebuild the world from scratch. They only need to build the part that makes that world different.

The internet went through a similar process. At first every company built its own servers, payment systems, and handled almost everything itself. Then cloud appeared, payment platforms appeared, authentication services appeared. Layer by layer, infrastructure was peeled off the app so developers could spend more time on the problem they actually wanted to solve.

Maybe interfaces are entering a similar process now.

Instead of every startup building another app with a home page, sidebar, profile, notifications, and a pile of nearly identical screens, many future products may simply be a bot, a community, and a very smart system running behind them.

## Maybe we do not need another app

He used to think that building a product had to start with a website or mobile app. Now he is not so sure.

If a product needs a camera, maps, graphics, or special interactions, a dedicated app still makes sense. But for products whose essence is connecting people, exchanging information, learning, consulting, hiring, dating, or building community, creating another app may only be asking users to install one more icon on their phone.

Discord already has identity. It already has communication. It already has notifications. It already has community. It already has a large user base. And most importantly, it has an API broad enough for developers to place a different brain behind those conversations.

AI may be making software smarter, but the paradox is that their interfaces may become simpler.

In the end, the oldest interface humans have ever had was never a screen, a menu, or a set of buttons.

It has always been a conversation.
